Chair: Welcome to today's debate. The motion is: This House would ban single-use plastics. I now call upon the first Government speaker.
Government 1: Thank you, chair. Single-use plastics are choking our oceans and poisoning the food chain. We propose a phased ban over three years, with subsidised biodegradable alternatives for small businesses. This protects the environment without destroying livelihoods.
Opposition 1: Point of information.
Government 1: Accepted.
Opposition 1: Isn't it true that a ban would hit low-income families hardest, since the alternatives you mention are far more expensive?
Government 1: No, because our subsidy scheme specifically caps the price of alternatives for households below the median income, so the burden falls on producers, not families.
Government 1: To continue, the environmental cost of inaction dwarfs any short-term inconvenience. Micro-plastics are now found in human blood.
Opposition 1: Thank you, chair. The Government's plan sounds noble but is unworkable. Subsidies cost money, and they have not told us who pays. Meanwhile, thousands of jobs in packaging manufacturing vanish overnight.
Government 1: Point of information.
Opposition 1: Go ahead.
Government 1: If the environmental damage is as costly as scientists say, isn't doing nothing the far more expensive option in the long run?
Opposition 1: That assumes the ban actually reduces damage, which you have not proven; plastic may simply be replaced by equally harmful substitutes.
Government 2: On that point.
Opposition 1: No, thank you, I will continue. The point is that innovation, not prohibition, solves pollution.
Government 2: Thank you, chair. My colleague has shown the environmental stakes. Let me address the economics directly. The packaging jobs the Opposition fears are already declining; our transition fund retrains those workers for the growing green-materials sector.
Opposition 2: Point of information.
Government 2: Not right now, thank you.
Government 2: A managed transition creates more jobs than it loses, as we saw when Europe phased out CFCs.
Opposition 2: Thank you. Retraining schemes have a poor track record, and the Government offers no evidence this one differs. Prohibition drives consumption underground rather than eliminating it.
Chair: Time. We now move to the reply speeches.
Government 1: In reply: the core clash is whether we value short-term convenience over long-term survival. The Opposition never disputed that plastics harm us; they only asked who pays, and we answered. The motion stands.
